The Percussive Legacies of Hip Hop Feminism

Hip Hop and American Culture

2026

Hip hop is more than just music, it's a form of embodied knowledge, performance, and cultural memory. Contemporary artists including Rapsody and Doechii inherit a tradition of performance established by Lauryn Hill. Rather than imitating Hill, they expand hip hop feminism into a new generation through performances and public self-definition that Hill paved the way for. Black women artists are forced to negotiate perceptions of self with commercial success, while maintaining artistic authenticity. Rapsody and Doechii build on opportunities that Hill created to redefine Black womanhood for a contemporary audience.Rapsody and Doechii demonstrate that hip hop feminism continues through performance rather than simple imitation. Music videos, live performances, and interviews transform Black bodies into an archive of Black history while using contradiction, vulnerability, and artistic experimentation to extend the possibilities first explored by Lauryn Hill.

Both Rapsody and Doechii demonstrate Batiste’s argument that performance is a form of embodied knowledge by using their bodies, voices, and visual symbolism to communicate histories that cannot be reduced to written text. Batiste defines performance as a way of knowing and preserving history, shaping identity, and expressing the Black experience. Black identity is constantly performed and does not remain fixed, making performance a way of being in which memory, identity and history are communicated through movement, sound, and embodiment (Batiste, 137-139). This understanding of performance is demonstrated in Rapsody’s Nina, where she situated herself within generations of influential Black women such as Nina Simone, Lauryn Hill, and Angela Davis. Rapsody’s performance does not simply reference these women, it embodies the history they represent. The minimalist music video style, direct eye contact, and lack of spectacle emphasize the way that Black artistic traditions are preserved through performance and collective memory. (RapsodyVEVO) Rapsody’s performance becomes an act of remembrance that also positions Rapsody within a lineage of influential Black feminists. In doing this she demonstrates the concept of autoperformativity, both performing her own identity while seeing herself within a longer historical tradition. Doechii also illustrates performance as embodied knowledge by exploring building identity through theatrical expression. During her performance on The Late Show with Stephen Colbert, she uses synchronized choreography, interconnected braids, and minimal staging to represent collective identity. Rather than relying on written explanation, Doechii’s performance visually communicated themes of community and lineage (Inman). Doechii’s performance is highly self conscious, controlled, and carefully choreographed to convey the message. This level of control in performance connects to Batiste’s concept of autoperformativity because she is not just performing for the audience, but actively trying to shape the version of herself being presented. Through this, the performance becomes a scene where identity is being shaped through performance and not simply displayed. Both artists perform very differently, but both reflect Batiste’s idea that identity is created through performance. Both artists transform performance into a vessel that carries Black feminist history through movement, music, and visuals rather than simply written histories

While performances present artistic identities, interviews reveal the tensions that inform those performances. Tensions revealed in interviews further demonstrate Durham's concept of percussive feminism. Durham details that percussive feminism argues that tension and contradiction aren’t inherently negative because hip hop operates in a ‘gray area’ where tension creates new dialogue and knowledge (Durham, 217). Both artists reject the idea that authenticity means choosing between oppositional identities. When interviewed Rapsody discusses how in order for her to achieve personal growth she had to “unplug from the matrix” to reconnect with her humanness and vulnerability. Through this, she frames her escape from the matrix taking place in the hair salon, a space where Black communities gather, build relationships and share knowledge (EBRO, 3:30-5:00). Rapsody’s focus on a space that preserves Black history, identity and experience functions as what Durham describes as a public pedagogy. Shifting Black women from the margins to the center as producers of knowledge rather than simply as consumers of culture positions Rapsody’s mention of the hair salon as a place where Black feminist knowledge is continually created, shared, and sustained. Doechii embraces a different form of contradiction by refusing to define herself through a single artistic identity. Through her Apple Music interview, she discusses internal conflict about what type of artist she wanted to be before realizing that categorization was distracting her from defining what story she wanted to tell (Apple Music, 12:03-15:51). Doechii explains that she feels more obligated to stay true to herself and the traditions of hip hop through lyricism, vulnerability, and storytelling rather than simply achieving commercial success (Apple Music, 23:28-26:45). Both Rapsody and Doechii refuse to choose between identities that the music industry treats as opposites. Their continued performance and resistance highlight that authenticity comes from continual self definition rather than conformity. Together these interviews validate Durham's argument that hip hop feminism doesn’t rely on the elimination of contradiction because it transforms competing expectations into opportunities for artistic growth and Black feminist knowledge. The interviews reveal that hip hop feminism exists not only in music but also within the everyday work of negotiating identity, industry, and community.

Rather than simply following in Lauryn Hill’s footsteps, Rapsody and Doechii further expand the possibilities that Hill created for Black women in hip hop. Morgan argues that Lauryn Hill’s greatest legacy was not simply musical, but cultural, transforming what Black womanhood could look like in and outside of hip hop (Morgan, Ch. 4). Hill’s demonstration that Black women did not have to conform to narrow expectations of beauty, femininity, motherhood or artistry in order to be successful challenged the idea that Black women must choose between authenticity and commercial success. Instead, she demonstrated that contradictory identities can coexist, and paved the way for future female artists to define themselves. Rapsody builds on Hill’s legacy by focusing her artistry in Black historical memory. Through Nina and her interview with Ebro, she consistently centers Black women, cultural heritage, and collective knowledge while presenting hip hop as a tradition that carries history through performance and identity (EBRO, 6:13-7:00). Doechii extends Hill’s legacy differently. Rather than presenting herself in Hill’s image, she embraces musical experimentation, theatrical performance, and identity changes while remaining committed to hip hop tradition. Her performances reject the idea that Black women should fit into predetermined musical categories, and her interviews emphasize that storytelling and honesty are more important than industry expectations (Apple Music, 22:33- 27:48). Though Rapsody and Doechii approach hip hop through different artistic and stylistic lenses, both of their performances are a result of the freedom Morgan argues Hill made possible. Lauryn Hill did not leave a blueprint for future Black women to imitate, rather she left a space where Black women had permission to continually redefine themselves. The lasting legacy of hip hop feminism is not found in reproducing Lauryn Hill, but in extending her reach of self definition, autonomy, and the continual redefinition of Black womanhood.

All together, Batiste, Durham, Morgan, and the artists themselves, demonstrate that hip hop feminism is not just a critique of sexism within the hip hop community, but an ongoing practice of creating new possibilities for Black womanhood through performance and self definition. Rapsody and Doechii inherit Lauryn Hill’s legacy not through reproduction, but through her permission to pursue authenticity, vulnerability and autonomy. Both artists' performances reveal that hip hop continues to function as a space where Black women are able to negotiate identity and challenge dominant narratives while producing knowledge. Lauryn Hill’s greatest accomplishment did not just lie within her music, but in the space she created for future generations of Black women to define themselves on their own terms. Rather than reproducing Lauryn Hill's image, Rapsody and Doechii used the permission that Lauryn created. Through these contemporary artists its clear Lauryn Hills greatest legacy is expanding what Black women in hip hop are allowed to be. Through embodied performance, public pedagogy, and artistic contradiction, they continue to reinvent hip hop feminism for a new generation.
